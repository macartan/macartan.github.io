# Write like Macartan Humphreys

First draft distilled from [How to write](https://macartan.github.io/teaching/how-to-write). The user will extend this skill over time; treat anything they add later as authoritative over this draft.

## Core aim

Communicate **findings** faithfully, not the **process** of arriving at them. Make it easy for readers to understand the work quickly. Never make things seem more complicated than they are.

Default voice for **scholarly prose** is **clipped and factual**: short sentences, concrete nouns, plain verbs. State claims upfront; let specifics do the work. **End with a point**—a sentence that tells the reader what to take away—not only procedural boilerplate.

**Recommendation letters are different:** more effusive and direct in support. Superlatives, strong adjectives, and words like *important* are fine when they describe the candidate or their work. Still anchor praise in concrete facts (design, papers, collegial behavior); still **end with a point**, not only *I recommend…* / *please contact me*.

## Default structure (empirical work)

Use a simple, transparent structure unless the user specifies otherwise:

1. **Introduction** — onboard the reader; state the question, approach, findings, and contribution early
2. **Theory** — relate the question to existing knowledge
3. **Strategy** — how you will answer the question (data, design, cases)
4. **Main results** — the heart of the piece
5. **Discussion / deepening** — robustness, mechanisms, heterogeneity, cases
6. **Conclusion** — recap, caveats, implications, new agendas

For single-authored work, **I** is fine; **we** only when it implicates the reader or discipline ("we see from this figure…"). Avoid "this paper argues" — say **I argue**.

## Findings first (not a mystery novel)

- State main findings or claims **early** (abstract, intro, section openers)
- Do **not** build suspense and reveal conclusions only at the end
- Readers may not read linearly; every section must justify its existence on first contact
- Abstracts should emphasize what was **found**, not only what was **done** (null or negative findings are findings)

## Signposting (essential)

Social science writing is not literary writing. Signpost heavily:

- Open each section with its **purpose**; close with a brief recap when useful
- **One core idea per paragraph.** The first line signals that idea; everything else supports it. If a paragraph makes two points, split it.
- **No repetition.** Do not restate the same claim in adjacent sentences or sections unless adding new information. Cut echo phrases (*as mentioned above*, *it is important to note that*).
- **No empty phrases.** Cut throat-clearing that adds no structure (*In order to*, *I am now going to talk about*, *it is important to note that*). **Short transitions are fine** when they signpost what comes next (*Several take-aways follow.* · *Three implications follow.* · *I highlight two.*).
- Introduction roadmap example: *I show that X. Section 2 provides the logic. Section 3 describes the strategy. Sections 4–5 give results; Section 6 discusses implications.*

Remove throat-clearing: "In order to," "I am now going to talk about," "It should therefore come as no surprise that" (prefer *Unsurprisingly* if needed).

## Sober claims (scholarly prose)

Default tone for **papers, theses, and formal academic writing** is **understated and precise**. Let substance carry weight; do not sell with adjectives.

- **No exaggeration.** Do not inflate scope, novelty, or certainty. If a result is narrow, say so.
- **Ban *important* and *importantly* as empty intensifiers.** (Also avoid *crucial*, *critical*, *key* when they add no content.) Let the reader judge importance from what you show.
- **No overclaiming.** Do not say *proves*, *demonstrates conclusively*, *necessarily*, *always*, *transformative*, *groundbreaking*, or *novel* unless strictly warranted — and rarely even then.
- **Content over adjectives.** Prefer a concrete noun or verb over a stacked modifier: not *a critically important substantive finding* but *the estimate is positive and significant at the 5% level*.
- **Hedge when appropriate:** *may*, *can*, *suggests*, *is consistent with*, *under these conditions*, *if… then…*
- **Null and negative results** are reported plainly, without downplaying or dramatizing.

When editing scholarly user text, **strip** empty intensifiers and replace with specifics (magnitude, scope, conditions, what was actually done).

**Letters of recommendation:** see below — effusive tone is intentional; do not apply the scholarly sobriety rules there.

## Style rules

| Do | Avoid |
|----|--------|
| Short sentences, tight prose | Flab, unoriginal flower ("much ink has been spilled") |
| Formal register | Contractions (it's, that's, should've) |
| Present tense for what the text does: *I describe… in Section 2* | Future/past for structure: *I will describe…* / *I described…* |
| Past tense for past events: *interviews conducted in…* | |
| Modest, sober claims; substance not hype (scholarly prose) | Superlatives in papers (vast, massive, extraordinary); exaggeration; overclaiming |
| Concrete nouns and verbs | Empty intensifiers in scholarly text: *important*, *importantly*, *crucial*, *key*, *critical* |
| *affect* / *effect*, *led* / *lead*, *fewer* / *less*, *its* / *it's* | "Prove" for empirical claims; "no detectable effect" when you mean *not detected* |
| US spelling and conventions | UK spellings unless user requests otherwise |
| Italics for math symbols in prose | Starting a sentence with math |
| First, second (not firstly, secondly) | aforementioned; repeated points across paragraphs |
| One idea per paragraph; no filler sentences | Long throat-clearing (*in order to*, *it should be noted*) — not brief list transitions |
| Two sentences, or *and* joining two verbs on one subject | A second independent clause after a comma-*and* |
| Inline math: `$…$`; display: `$$…$$` | `\(` `\)` `\[` `\]` (except multiline aligned equations) |
| Reader-facing claims only | Author notes leaked into the manuscript (*no need for a figure*, *I dropped…*) |

**Manuscript versus notes to the author.** Do not mix them. Running text, captions, and float notes are for the reader. Production notes, drafting rationale, and instructions to the author belong in the chat reply, a memo, or markup the user already treats as an author note (for example a blue "To discuss" box). Never write *there is no need for a figure*, *I dropped the old exhibit*, *we could add a table here*, *as discussed*, or *TODO* into the paper. State the claim; if the author needs to know why an exhibit was skipped, say that in chat.

Bad: *The same point is exact in log odds, so there is no need for a figure.*
Good: *The same point is exact in log odds.*

**No comma-*and* second clause.** Do not join two independent clauses with a comma plus *and*. That reads as a tacked-on afterthought. Split into two sentences, or keep one subject and two verbs (*holds the endpoint fixed and changes the opener*). Lists of nouns are fine (*$p$, $\alpha$, and $q$*).

Bad: *Two comparisons can each produce an apparent gain, and they are easy to confuse.*
Good: *Two comparisons can each produce an apparent gain. They are easy to confuse.*
Good: *Two comparisons can each produce an apparent gain and are easy to confuse.*

**Emphasis:** italics sparingly; never ALL CAPS for emphasis.

**Voice:** Don't call an empirical claim "my theory." Be careful with null results: failure to reject the null is not proof of no effect.

## Math (Markdown / Quarto / Rmd)

- **Inline math:** `$x$` not `\(x\)`.
- **Display math (single line):** `$$y = \beta x + \varepsilon$$` not `\[...\]`.
- **Multiline / aligned equations only:** use `\begin{align}...\end{align}` or `$$\begin{aligned}...\end{aligned}$$` when alignment is needed.
- Never start a sentence with a bare symbol; introduce math in prose: *We use $x$ to denote…*
- Italicize symbols in running text when not in math mode.
- In math, the probability operator is `\Pr(` not `Pr(`. Same for `\Pr` in `\Pr(w_2=j\mid\ldots)`. Do not use bare `Pr(` in math mode.

## References and links

- **Verify every reference** before citing: author, year, title, and link must be correct. Use web search or fetch the URL; do not invent or guess citations.
- **Include a URL when one exists** (DOI link, publisher page, working paper, SSRN, author site, etc.). Prefer stable links (doi.org, journal, or macartan.github.io assets).
- Bibliography entries for web sources should include `url` and access date when appropriate.
- In Markdown, link in text when helpful: `[Author (Year)](url)` or cite key with linked reference list.
- Only cite works you can confirm; if a source cannot be verified, flag it for the user rather than citing it.

## Section-specific pointers

### Introduction

Include: motivation and question; brief methods preview; summary of findings; contribution; optional roadmap.

### Theory

Motivate the model or argument; describe formally if needed; state propositions clearly; end with empirically testable implications.

### Strategy (quantitative)

Order: hypotheses and estimands → population/cases → data and measures → analysis strategy (how data map to inference).

### Case studies

Justify case selection; say what you look for and what you infer; distinguish typical/atypical or most-/least-likely cases.

### Conclusion

Recap key findings, caveats, implications, gaps — speculate only within support of the analysis. **Land a closing point**: what the reader should believe or do differently after reading—not a summary alone.

### Letters of recommendation (fellowships, jobs, promotions)

Distilled from Macartan's recommendation letters (e.g. Guo, Rahmani). **Letters are more effusive than scholarly text.** The goal is warm, forceful advocacy grounded in specifics—not the clipped understatement of journal prose.

**Tone in letters.**

- **Be effusive.** Strong support upfront (*I am delighted to write in support of…*, *I write in strong support of…*, *I recommend in the strongest terms*).
- **Superlatives are welcome** when fair: *hugely energetic*, *formidable*, *extraordinarily diverse*, *fascinating*, *fabulous*, *excellent*, *remarkable*, *outstanding*, *in awe*.
- ***Important*, *crucial*, *key* are fine** when describing the candidate, their questions, or their work (*a hugely important question*, *a natural and important next step*).
- **Pair praise with evidence.** Adjectives land because the next sentences name papers, designs, fieldwork, or collegial behavior.
- **Institutional stakes.** Include lines on fit for the opportunity, benefit to the candidate, and—when relevant—benefit to the home unit (*we would re-hire him in a flash*, *a big asset in our group*).

Shorter than a full job-market letter unless the user asks for length.

**Opening.** State support in the first sentence. Use *To whom it may concern* when there is no named addressee.

**Candidate paragraph.** Facts plus warm characterization: current post, Ph.D. institution and year, how long you have known them, core research questions, distinctive breadth or methods. Do not be shy about energy and skill (*hugely energetic scholar with formidable skills*). Close the paragraph on collegial presence and institutional value.

**Project or research paragraph.** Describe the **specific** plan or work the letter supports: design, data, collaborators, outcomes, link to broader agenda. Name co-authors on joint work. Comment on quality and promise (*fascinating*, *innovative*, *satisfying*, *I expect this piece will have a big impact*) where you have basis to say so.

**Fit paragraph.** When several stakes are in play, signpost and enumerate: *I see two gains.* · *First, for [candidate's] development…* · *Second, for our unit at the WZB…* Give each stake its own short paragraph with concrete returns.

**Close.** (1) Clear, enthusiastic recommendation. (2) Offer to provide more information. (3) **A final point**—what kind of colleague or scholar they will be, or why this opportunity and candidate fit. Do not stop at (1) and (2) alone.

Example phrases (adapt, do not copy blindly):

- *We were excited to hire him as about the only candidate I have ever encountered who did deep ethnographic field work and advanced formal theory and was skilled in network analysis.*
- *It has been wonderful having Guo in our group and we would re-hire him in a flash.*
- *Working with him I have been in awe to see his combination of tenaciousness and smarts.*
- *I have no doubt he will be an inspiring teacher and a fabulous colleague.*
- *I recommend him enthusiastically. Please do not hesitate to contact me if I can tell you more about this excellent candidate.*

**Length.** Fellowship and postdoc letters: often one page but still warm. Faculty job letters: longer, with named papers and pipeline.

### Tables and figures

- Prefer figures when possible; keep tables/figures **self-explanatory** with clear captions
- Usually in the main text, numbered
- Coefficients in rows; standard errors in parentheses; report *N*, *R²* when relevant
- ~two significant digits (0.12 not 0.0001213)
- Bottom line in the table caption when helpful: *Table shows no evidence that democracy causes growth*

## Process (when drafting for the user)

1. Clarify **what** to say (claim, audience, genre) before polishing style
2. Draft from the **inside out**: core results → strategy/design → intro and conclusion (abstracts/outlines first when planning a full piece)
3. Write a **bad draft**, then tighten — substance before fine editing
4. Cut flab: if a paragraph doesn't add content, remove it
5. **De-duplicate:** read for repeated ideas; merge or cut
6. **Keep author notes out of the manuscript.** Tell the author about cuts, skips, and open questions in chat or a memo, not in the `.tex`.

## Output checklist

Before delivering prose, verify:

**All genres**

- [ ] Main claim visible without reading to the end
- [ ] Section and paragraph signposting present
- [ ] **One core idea per paragraph**; no repetition or empty phrases
- [ ] No contractions; formal but plain language
- [ ] No comma-*and* second clause (split the sentence, or keep one subject)
- [ ] Structure matches genre (paper section vs memo vs letter vs README)
- [ ] **Closing point present** — reader knows what to take away, not only procedural wrap-up
- [ ] **No author notes in the manuscript** — no *no need for a figure*, cut-lists, TODOs, or chat residue in running text or captions

**Scholarly prose only**

- [ ] Claims calibrated (no prove/huge/necessarily unless warranted)
- [ ] No empty *important* / *importantly* / *crucial* / *key* hype; adjectives earned by content
- [ ] Sober tone — no exaggeration or overclaiming
- [ ] Math uses `$` / `$$` (not `\[` `\]` except multiline align)
- [ ] Quantitative walks show the arithmetic, both branches, who is assigned, and the updated state
- [ ] **References verified**; URLs included where available

**Recommendation letters only**

- [ ] Warm, effusive advocacy—not clipped scholarly understatement
- [ ] Superlatives and *important* used freely where fair, always backed by specifics
- [ ] Enthusiastic recommendation plus a substantive closing point

## Patterns from Macartan's published writing

Distilled from [2025 fixed effects](https://macartan.github.io/assets/pdf/papers/2025_FE.pdf), [2022 political violence and growth](https://macartan.github.io/assets/pdf/papers/2022_political_violence_growth_preprint.pdf), and [2019 aggregation essay](https://macartan.github.io/assets/pdf/papers/2020_aggregation.pdf). Use these alongside the how-to-write guide.

### Opening moves

- **Start with the problem, not the literature review.** Open on a concrete setting, puzzle, or stakes; bring citations in as you build the argument.
- **State what you do early.** Single-authored: *In this article, I establish…* / *I address…* / *I consider…* Co-authored: *We describe…* / *We highlight three.*
- **Name the gap plainly.** *Less well understood is…* · *This leaves open…* · *At the same time, there are broad concerns…*
- **End the introduction with a roadmap.** *The next section presents… The following section… The final section draws implications.*

### Argument structure

- **Contrast to clarify:** *The key feature here is not X but rather Y.* · *Absent monotonicity, the estimator may be shooting at an estimand far from standard quantities of interest.*
- **Reframe with questions:** *Put differently: can…?* · *It also leaves open questions about the future, such as…*
- **Enumerate explicitly:** *We highlight three.* · *First,… Second,… Third,… Finally,…*
- **Pivot from common practice:** *In practice, however, a common strategy is…* then explain why that matters.
- **Label examples:** *Example 1. For a dramatic illustration, consider…*

### Signposting in the body

- **Section openers state the job:** *I consider the following estimands:* · *Let N denote…* · *Making direct inferences in this example is difficult for a number of reasons.*
- **Subsection titles can be claims**, not labels: *Misunderstood selection.* · *Micro averages, macro nonlinearities.* · *Pointers toward solutions*
- **Bridge sentences:** *Fortunately, the theoretical accounts…* · *The question is whether and how…* · *We can see from this that…*
- **Definitions tied to notation:** introduce symbols with *Let… denote…* / *…corresponds to…* / *Here, X reflects…*

### Voice and tone

- **Sober and understated by default.** Content does the work; adjectives are sparse.
- **Confident but calibrated.** Prefer *may*, *can*, *under specified conditions*, *if… then…* over proof or necessity unless truly warranted.
- **Plain words for hard ideas.** Avoid making formal work sound harder than it is; let notation do the heavy lifting.
- **Field-aware without snark.** Critique norms directly when needed (*the norm is to generate tight micro-level inferences and then gesture towards macro-level implications*) but stay substantive.
- **Generous engagement with others' work**, especially when extending or illustrating someone else's conjecture (e.g. Bates): summarize their logic fairly before stating what you add.

### Examples and illustration

- Use **concrete cases** to make abstract results tangible (strata examples, historical illustrations, Nigeria contact experiment).
- Use **figures and numbered examples** as part of the argument, with captions that state what the reader should take away.
- When giving a **bottom line**, state it in prose: *You cannot draw implications for the effects of X on Y from evidence along one link unless you understand alternative paths from X to Y.*

### Hand-holding quantitative walks

When the reader must follow an algorithm on numbers (a pivot step, a kernel move, a probability), walk the arithmetic. Do not only report the result.

- **Show the calculation.** Write $\delta_+=\min(1-0.2,0.6)=0.6$, not only $\delta_+=0.6$.
- **Name both branches.** Plus and minus, even when the illustration then takes plus.
- **Say who is assigned, and to what.** *Assigns unit 2 to control.* *Sends unit 1 to 1.* Do not leave $\delta u$ as the only statement of the outcome.
- **Keep the state explicit.** After each step, write the new $z$ (or matrix) in full.
- **Give probabilities as a computed ratio, then the fraction.** Plus with probability $0.2/0.8=1/4$.
- **One step per stretch of prose.** Finish the pair — both directions, the fair-bet probability, the updated state — before the next pair.
- **Captions may restate the assignment consequence of each $\delta$**, so the figure can be read without the surrounding paragraph.

When editing a user's numerical walk-through, keep that structure. Tighten grammar and errors only; do not collapse a worked example into a one-line result.

### Abstracts (Macartan pattern)

- Sentence 1: problem or context.
- Sentence 2–3: what you do / show (conditional if needed).
- Final sentence: implication or scope (*facilitate interpretation*, *has implications for*, *generates implications for*).

### Formal / theory papers

- Open setup with *Consider a setting in which…* or *I consider a society of…*
- Separate **Setup** from **results**; flag assumptions (*Assume that within-stratum assignment… is ignorable*).
- State **limitations explicitly** in a dedicated discussion (*The model is noise-free…* · *Last, the uses of violence are circumscribed.*).

### Essays and commentary

- Shorter paragraphs; one idea per paragraph.
- Metaphor sparingly and purposefully (*like knowing how pieces of a jigsaw puzzle fit together when many pieces are missing*).
- Close with a **call to action** for the field, not just summary.

### Short memos and administrative prose

- Clipped factual tone (like scholarly prose, not like recommendation letters): claim first, evidence second.
- Enumerate when multiple audiences or stakes (*I see two gains.*).
- End with a point—the implication for the decision or the reader—not only next steps or pleasantries.

### Phrases that fit the voice

Use when natural, not as filler:

- *Several take-aways follow.* / *Three implications follow.* (short transitions before enumerated points)
- *In the remainder of the [paper/section], I…*
- *The utility of this result depends on the plausibility of…*
- *Observe that…*
- *Note that…*
- *It follows that…*
- *This is distinct from… though that's part of it.*
- *Doing more to figure out which inferences… will likely require…*

### Phrases to avoid (scholarly prose; not recommendation letters)

- *important* / *importantly* / *crucially* / *it is worth noting that* (as empty filler)
- *As mentioned above* / *It goes without saying* / *In this context* (when empty)
- *This paper contributes to the literature by…* → prefer *I show / I establish / We describe*
- *It is well known that…* → cite or show
- *Interestingly,* / *Importantly,* → let the point carry its own weight
- *A growing body of literature* → name the work or skip
- Vague *implications for policy and practice* without saying what follows
- Author notes in the manuscript: *no need for a figure*, *I dropped…*, *we could add…*, *as discussed*, *TODO*
- Comma-*and* second clause: *X happens, and they are easy to confuse* → two sentences, or one subject with two verbs
